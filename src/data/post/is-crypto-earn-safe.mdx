Exchanges make earning interest on crypto look as simple as a savings account: pick a coin, click "Subscribe" and rewards show up every day. But a crypto earn product is not a bank deposit. There's no government deposit insurance, and the interest is, in the end, a payment for taking risk.

You can still use them. Just know what can go wrong, and pick platforms and amounts with that in mind.

## Where the yield comes from

Interest has to come from somewhere. On most platforms it comes from one of these:

- **Lending.** Your coins are lent to borrowers, usually traders who want leverage. They pay interest, and the platform passes part of it on to you. This is where most stablecoin yield comes from, which is why "USDT staking" is really USDT lending.
- **Staking.** Coins like ETH or SOL pay rewards for helping secure their network. The exchange stakes on your behalf and keeps a cut.
- **DeFi protocols.** On platforms like Aave, interest comes from borrowers in an on-chain lending market, and the rate moves with demand.
- **Marketing.** New-user offers and boosted rates are often paid out of the platform's own budget to attract deposits. They're capped and short for that reason (see [what new-user offers really pay](/guides/new-user-crypto-earn-offers)).

If a rate is far above everything else, with no cap and no clear source, ask who is paying for it.

## The risks

### 1. The platform freezes withdrawals or fails

This is the biggest risk, and it has happened before. In 2022 several large lenders and exchanges froze withdrawals and went bankrupt, including Celsius, Voyager, BlockFi and FTX. Customers waited years and many got back only part of their money.

The main lesson comes from Celsius. In January 2023 a US bankruptcy judge ruled that the crypto in Celsius Earn accounts belonged to Celsius, not to its customers, because the terms of use transferred ownership. That made Earn customers unsecured creditors.

Many earn products work the same way: to lend your coins out, the platform needs the right to use them. Read the terms for words like "transfer of title", "you grant us the right to lend" or "rehypothecation".

### 2. Hacks

Exchanges hold huge amounts of crypto, which makes them a target. In February 2025 attackers stole about $1.5 billion in ETH from Bybit, the largest crypto hack on record. Bybit covered the loss with loans and purchases and kept withdrawals running. A smaller platform might not have been able to.

### 3. The stablecoin loses its dollar peg

Earning 5% on a stablecoin assumes it stays worth $1. That isn't guaranteed:

- In May 2022 the algorithmic stablecoin UST collapsed to almost zero within days.
- In March 2023 USDC fell to about $0.87 for a weekend, after it emerged that part of its cash reserves were held at the failed Silicon Valley Bank. It recovered once the deposits were secured.

A year of interest doesn't make up for a 13% drop. Prefer large, well-backed stablecoins, and don't put everything in one.

### 4. Smart-contract risk (DeFi)

On DeFi platforms there's no company holding your coins, but there's code, and code can have bugs. Lending protocols have been exploited for millions through flaws in their contracts or in the price feeds they rely on. Long-running, audited protocols have a better track record, but an audit lowers the risk without removing it.

DeFi also makes you responsible for your own wallet. A lost seed phrase or a signature on a phishing site can't be reversed.

### 5. You can't get your coins out when you want

Fixed terms keep your coins until the term ends, and leaving early usually costs you the interest earned so far. Flexible products can be delayed too, when everyone withdraws at once (which is exactly when you'd most want out). On DeFi, a lending pool can run short of cash to pay out if most of its deposits are borrowed.

### 6. Price risk on non-stablecoins

Rewards are paid in the same coin you deposit. Earning 4% a year on SOL or ETH doesn't help much if the price falls 30%. On a coin like that, the interest is a small extra on top of a price that can swing a lot.

### 7. Rates and terms change

Rates on flexible products can change daily, and boosted rates usually apply only to a small first amount. Platforms can also close a product, or close it in your country, with little notice. Treat any rate as today's rate. It can change tomorrow.

## No deposit insurance

Bank deposits in most countries are protected by a government scheme up to a limit. Crypto earn products are not. Some exchanges keep their own emergency funds, such as Binance's SAFU fund, but they're voluntary. No law makes the company pay out of them.

## What proof of reserves does and doesn't show

After FTX, many exchanges began publishing proof of reserves: a snapshot showing that the coins in their wallets cover customer balances, often with a "Merkle tree" that lets you check your own balance was counted.

It's a good sign, and a platform that doesn't publish one deserves extra caution. But it only shows one moment, and the next week could look different. It also usually lists assets without all the liabilities, such as loans the exchange has taken. So unless an independent auditor reviews the full balance sheet, it doesn't prove the platform is solvent.

## Checklist before you deposit

1. Who runs it, and for how long? Prefer large platforms with a long track record and a clear legal entity.
2. Does it publish proof of reserves, and is it recent?
3. What do the terms say about ownership? Look for transfer of title or the right to lend your coins.
4. Where does the rate come from: lending, staking or a temporary promotion?
5. Flexible or locked? What happens if you need the coins early?
6. Is the rate capped? How much of your deposit actually earns the headline rate?
7. Is the product available in your country, and is the platform licensed where you live?
8. For DeFi: how long has the protocol run, has it been audited, and how much is deposited?

## How to lower the risk

- Spread it out. Don't keep everything on one platform or in one stablecoin.
- Only deposit what you could live with being frozen for months.
- Prefer flexible products unless the extra yield of locking is clearly worth it.
- Keep long-term holdings in your own wallet if you don't need them earning.
- Secure your account. Use an authenticator app for 2FA and turn on withdrawal address whitelisting. Log in from a bookmark, never from a link in an email or message.
